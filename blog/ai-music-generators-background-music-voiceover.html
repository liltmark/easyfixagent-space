How to Pick Background Music for Videos Without Drowning the Voiceover

October 27, 2026 · Music · 6 min read

Good background music should be felt more than noticed. The moment a viewer has to strain to hear what you are saying, the music has stopped doing its job — no matter how good the track sounds on its own. Picking the right track is less about taste and more about giving the voice room to breathe, matching the mood of the scene, and keeping the mix simple enough that it works on a phone speaker as well as headphones.

1. Start With the Voice, Not the Song

Before you browse a single track, listen to your voiceover on its own. Is it deep or bright? Fast or slow? Warm or matter-of-fact? A busy, melodic track will fight a fast voice, while a sparse, steady track leaves space for every word. If your narration carries a lot of information, you need music that stays out of the way — fewer instruments, a steady rhythm, and no sudden surprises. Save the dramatic, full-band tracks for sections with no talking, like an intro, a transition, or a montage.

2. Choose Simpler Tracks Than You Think You Need

3. Get the Volume Balance Right

As a starting point, set the music far lower than feels natural. Many editors begin with the music around 15 to 25 percent of the voice level, then adjust by ear. The voice should stay clearly on top at all times, even during louder musical moments. If your editor supports it, use keyframe automation: dip the music slightly whenever someone speaks, and let it rise a little in pauses or silent gaps. That gentle movement sounds professional and keeps the mix from feeling flat or muddy.

4. Watch Out for Frequency Clashes

Voices and music often compete in the same frequency range, roughly where human speech lives. A track heavy in mid-range guitars, pianos, or synths can mask the voice even at low volume. If your editor has a basic equalizer, try gently reducing the music in the mid-range while the voice is playing. You do not need to be an audio engineer for this — a small cut makes a noticeable difference. Tracks built around bass and soft high-end textures, with less going on in the middle, tend to sit under speech more easily.

5. Match the Mood, Not Just the Genre

A cheerful ukulele track is not automatically right for a cheerful video, and a serious piano piece is not automatically right for a serious one. Think about energy and pace instead. A calm tutorial needs steady, unhurried music. A fast product demo can handle a brighter tempo. A heartfelt story needs space and warmth. Listen to the first ten seconds of a track and ask: does this feel like the video I just watched? If you have to talk yourself into it, keep looking. The right track usually feels obvious within a few seconds.

6. Edit the Music to Fit the Video

Do not just drop a three-minute song under a ninety-second video and hope for the best. Cut the track to fit, fade it in at the start, and fade it out at the end so it never stops abruptly. If a track has a loud chorus in the middle, you can often cut around it or lower that section. Looping a quiet section is perfectly fine — viewers rarely notice a clean loop, but they always notice a track that ends mid-phrase. Keep your edits on the beat where possible, and the music will feel like it was made for your video.

7. Test on a Phone Speaker

Most people will watch your video on a phone, often without headphones, and phone speakers hide bass while exaggerating harsh sounds. A mix that sounds perfect in headphones can turn muddy or tinny on a phone. Before you publish, play the video back on your phone at normal volume, in a normal room, not a quiet studio. If you can follow every word without turning the volume up and down, your balance is right. If not, lower the music further before you blame the voice recording.

8. Check the License Before You Publish

A great track is useless if you cannot legally use it. Always confirm what the license covers: personal use, commercial use, YouTube, social media, or client work are often different permissions. Keep a note of where each track came from and what you are allowed to do with it. Royalty-free does not always mean free, and free does not always mean cleared for monetized videos. When in doubt, choose a track with clear terms, and keep the receipt or license page saved with your project files.

9. A Simple Workflow That Works Every Time

Here is a reliable order of operations: finish the voiceover first, then pick two or three candidate tracks, then place each one under the voice and listen on headphones and a phone. Choose the one that disappears most gracefully behind the words. Set the level low, add gentle dips under speech, fade the ends, and do a final phone-speaker check. Bottom line: The best background music is the track your audience barely notices — it supports the voice, matches the mood, and never asks to be the star.

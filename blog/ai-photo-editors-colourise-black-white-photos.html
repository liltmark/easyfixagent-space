Colourise Black-and-White Photos with AI: What Works and What to Watch

November 2, 2026 · AI Photo Editors · 5 min read
Written & tested by Muhammad Mudassir YaseenFounder, EasyFix Agent, every guide on this site is tested by hand before it is published.
🗓 Last updated: November 2026

A black and white family photo carries history in its tones, and seeing it in colour can feel like meeting the people in it for the first time. AI colourisation tools promise that transformation in one click, and the results can be striking. They can also be wrong in ways that matter: invented uniform colours, modern colour palettes on period clothing, and skin tones that look plausible but never existed. Colourisation is best treated as interpretation, not restoration of fact.

1. 1. Understand what the AI is actually doing

An AI colourisation tool does not recover the original colours of a photo. Those colours were never recorded, so the tool guesses based on patterns learned from millions of colour images: sky is usually blue, grass is usually green, skin falls in a range of tones. Guesses are often reasonable, but they are still guesses, and any detail the tool cannot infer from context becomes a statistically likely colour, not a true one.

That distinction shapes every decision that follows. A colourised photo is a new interpretation of an old image, useful for display and storytelling, but not evidence of how things actually looked. Keep the original black and white file safe and untouched, and treat every colourised version as a separate derivative. If anyone later asks what the original showed, the untouched file is the answer.

2. 2. Start with a clean, high quality scan

Colourisation amplifies whatever the scan contains, including dust, scratches, and blur. Scan the original at the highest practical resolution, clean obvious dust digitally, and fix major damage before colourising. Our guide to restoring old photos with AI covers careful repair, and our guide to fixing blurry and low light photos explains the limits of enhancement on poor sources.

Work on a copy, never on the only scan. If the colourisation goes wrong, you want to start again from the clean copy rather than rescanning a fragile original. Also check the scan for faded areas and uneven tones, because the AI reads those as content and may colour a stain as if it were part of the scene.

3. 3. Check skin tones, skies, and uniforms first

Three areas reveal colourisation errors fastest. Skin tones can shift too pink, too orange, or unnaturally even across a face. Skies can turn an implausible teal or gain colour banding. Uniforms, flags, and team colours can be guessed completely wrong, which matters most when the photo has historical or family significance. Inspect these areas at full size before sharing the result.

When you know the true colours from family knowledge or records, correct them by hand after the AI pass. A grandfather military uniform in a known regimental colour, or a house painted a remembered shade, deserves manual correction rather than an AI guess. Document any corrections you make, so future viewers know which colours came from memory and which came from the tool.

4. 4. Keep period colours honest

Modern colour palettes can quietly rewrite history. Bright synthetic colours on clothing from an era before those dyes existed, neon signage in a 1930s street scene, or vivid modern paint on a period building all look wrong to anyone who knows the era, and they mislead everyone else. If you know the period, favour muted, plausible tones over vivid ones, and be suspicious of any result that looks like a modern photograph.

Upscaling after colourisation can sharpen detail, but it can also invent texture that was never there. Our guide to photo upscaling explains when enlargement helps and when it invents false detail. For historical photos, modest enhancement beats aggressive sharpening, because invented detail becomes false history once the image circulates.

5. 5. Label colourised photos clearly

Whenever you share a colourised photo publicly, label it as colourised by AI. A simple caption such as colourised from the original black and white photograph prevents the image from being mistaken for a genuine colour original, which matters for family archives, local history projects, and social media alike. Honest labelling costs nothing and protects the photo value as a historical record.

For family sharing, include both versions side by side when possible. Relatives often enjoy the colour version emotionally while valuing the original as the true record. Presenting both respects both responses and avoids arguments later about which version is real. Keep filenames clear too, such as surname 1952 colourised, so the two versions never get confused in an archive.

6. 6. When colourisation is not the right choice

Some photos are better left black and white. Portraits where the lighting and contrast are the point, artistic photographs, and images with deep personal or memorial significance often lose power in colour. If the colourised version feels less moving than the original, trust that feeling. Not every old photo needs colour to be appreciated, and restraint is part of good stewardship.

Also avoid colourising photos of identifiable people for commercial or public use without considering consent and context, especially for recent historical images where relatives are alive. A colourised image can feel more immediate and personal than the original, which raises the stakes for respectful use. When in doubt, keep colourised versions for private family sharing and publish the original publicly.

Bottom line: Bottom line: Colourisation guesses colours it cannot recover, so start from a clean scan, check skin, skies and uniforms, correct what you know, and label the result honestly. That is how colour brings old photos to life without rewriting their history.

AI Music Licensing Explained: What Creators Can Actually Use

October 28, 2026 · AI Tools · 6 min read

AI music tools promise unlimited original tracks, but the licence attached to each track is what decides whether you can actually publish it. Many creators only discover the problem when a video is muted, claimed or taken down, long after the music sounded perfect in the edit. This guide explains the licensing questions that matter, in plain terms, so you can use AI music with confidence instead of hope.

1. Copyright basics every creator should know

Music normally involves two separate rights: the composition, meaning the melody and lyrics, and the recording, meaning the actual audio file. Traditional licences cover one or both, and using a song without the right licence can lead to a claim even if you bought the track elsewhere. AI-generated music complicates this, because there may be no human composer in the usual sense, and different tools make different claims about who owns the output. The practical lesson is simple: never assume a track is safe because it sounds original. Always check the tool's licence terms for your exact use.

2. Read the licence for your exact use case

Licences differ sharply between personal projects, monetised YouTube videos, client work and advertising, and a track cleared for one may not cover another. Free plans in particular often limit commercial use, cap audience size, or require attribution, and these limits are usually buried in terms pages rather than shown in the app. Before publishing, confirm four things: commercial use is allowed, your platform is covered, attribution is or is not required, and the licence survives if you cancel the plan. If any answer is unclear, keep the track for personal drafts only.

3. AI-generated is not automatically claim-proof

A common misunderstanding is that AI music cannot trigger copyright claims because no human wrote it. In practice, claims still happen: a generated track can resemble an existing song closely enough for an automated system to flag it, and platforms often act first and ask questions later. Some tools offer indemnity or a claim-dispute process, while others leave you to handle disputes alone. Our guide to getting usable, royalty-safe tracks explains how to choose tools with clearer licences and how to document your choices so a dispute is easier to win.

4. Keep proof of every track you publish

For every track you use publicly, save a small record: the tool name, the date generated, the prompt or settings, and a screenshot or copy of the licence terms that applied at the time. Store these in one folder per project. This takes two minutes per track and can save weeks if a platform flags your video months later, because you can show exactly where the music came from and under what terms. Creators who skip this step often find that the tool's terms have changed by the time they need them, and the old version is hard to recover.

5. Attribution: when it is required, do it properly

Some free music requires crediting the creator or the tool, and a vague mention in a description may not satisfy the licence. Check whether attribution must appear in the video itself, in the description, or both, and whether a link is required. Put the credit exactly where the licence asks, using the wording it specifies, rather than a casual thank-you. Skipping attribution on a track that requires it is a licence breach even if nobody notices at first, and it is an easy problem to avoid by copying the credit line when you download the track.

6. Platform rules are separate from licences

Even a properly licensed track can run into platform rules. YouTube's Content ID system, TikTok's commercial music library and Instagram's audio rules each treat music differently, and a track cleared for YouTube may still be restricted on another platform. Check each platform's music policy for your account type, especially business accounts, which face stricter limits than personal ones. When in doubt, use the platform's own audio library for that platform, and reserve AI-generated tracks for the platforms where you have confirmed the licence applies.

7. When paying is the sensible choice

Free AI music is fine for many projects, but there are times when a paid licence is simply cheaper than the risk: client work, advertisements, broadcast, or any video expected to earn significant revenue. A single paid track with a clear commercial licence removes doubt for everyone involved, including clients who may ask to see it. Think of it as insurance rather than expense. For everyday content, our advice on choosing background music that supports your voiceover helps you pick tracks wisely, and our narration guide covers keeping voice and music in balance once the licence is settled.

Bottom line: A track is only usable when its licence covers your platform, your commercial use and your audience — check that before you publish, and keep the proof.

How to Brief an AI Writing Tool So the First Draft Is Actually Usable

October 27, 2026 · Writing · 6 min read

Most disappointing AI drafts are not a tool problem; they are a brief problem. Give the tool a vague topic and you get vague writing back. Give it an audience, a purpose, a structure and a few facts, and the first draft starts to look like something you can actually edit.

1. Start with the Reader, Not the Topic

Before you type a prompt, decide who will read the piece and what they already know. A guide for complete beginners needs definitions and reassurance, while a guide for busy professionals needs direct answers and skimmable structure. Tell the tool both things explicitly: who the reader is, what they care about, and what level of detail they can handle. When the reader is fuzzy in your mind, the writing will be fuzzy on the page, because the tool has to guess at tone, depth and vocabulary. A single sentence about the reader does more work than a paragraph of adjectives about the style you want.

2. State the One Job the Piece Must Do

3. Give It the Structure Before It Writes

Do not let the tool invent the structure from scratch unless you genuinely have no preference. Supply the headings, or at least the order of ideas, and say roughly how long each section should be. This keeps the draft from spending half its length on background and rushing the part readers actually came for. A brief that says "short intro, then three steps with an example each, then common mistakes, then a checklist" will beat a brief that says "write an article about this topic" almost every time. If you are choosing between tools first, our roundup of free AI writing tools can help you pick one that handles structured briefs well.

4. Feed It Facts, Not Just Instructions

A writing tool cannot know your prices, your process, your examples or your customers, and it will invent plausible substitutes if you leave those gaps open. Paste the real facts into the brief: the numbers, the names, the steps in order, the example you want used, and anything that must not be claimed. This is the single biggest difference between a draft you can polish and a draft you have to rewrite, because fixing invented facts takes far longer than fixing awkward sentences. Treat the brief as the raw material and the tool as the shaping step, not the other way around.

5. Show It the Voice You Want

Describing a tone in abstract words like "professional but friendly" rarely produces that tone, because every writer interprets those words differently. Instead, paste a short sample of writing you like and say "match this voice", or name concrete rules: short sentences, second person, no jargon, one idea per paragraph. Concrete rules are testable, which means you can check the draft against them in seconds. If the tool drifts into corporate phrasing or flowery language, point to a specific sentence and say what to change, rather than repeating the abstract description and hoping for a different result.

6. Set Boundaries: Length, Format and What to Avoid

Good briefs say what not to do as clearly as what to do. State the target length, the format you need, and the phrases, claims or topics to avoid. If the piece is for a web page, say so, and ask for headings, short paragraphs and a scannable layout. If it must avoid hype, say that plainly and name the kind of wording you consider hype. Boundaries save you from the most common editing chore: cutting a long, confident draft down to the short, honest piece you actually wanted. The basics of good prompting in our prompt engineering basics guide apply here too, since a brief is really just a prompt with a job attached.

7. Ask for a Draft You Can Edit, Not a Finished Piece

Expecting a perfect final article from one prompt sets you up for disappointment and wastes the tool's real strength. Ask for a solid first draft with clear sections, and plan to do one focused editing pass yourself: check the facts, cut the filler, fix the voice, and add the examples only you can provide. When you judge a draft, judge it by how much of it survives your edit, not by whether it needed one. A draft that keeps sixty percent of its sentences after a careful edit is a success, and a brief that regularly produces that is worth reusing as a template.

8. Save the Briefs That Work

Once a brief produces a draft you like, save it as a reusable template with blanks for the topic, reader, facts and examples. Over a few weeks you will build a small library of briefs for the kinds of writing you do most: product descriptions, how-to guides, emails, social posts. Each template removes the blank-page problem and keeps your output consistent, which matters more than any single clever prompt. Review the templates occasionally and update the ones that have started producing stale or repetitive drafts, because tools change and your standards should change with them.

Bottom line: A good brief is half the writing — give the tool a reader, a job, a structure and real facts, and the first draft will finally be worth editing.

Write an About Page with AI Writing Tools: Tell Your Story Without the Awkward Bits

November 28, 2026 · AI Writing Tools · 5 min read
Written & tested by Muhammad Mudassir YaseenFounder, EasyFix Agent, every guide on this site is tested by hand before it is published.
🗓 Last updated: November 2026

Every business has a story, and the about page is where that story lives. The problem is that most owners stare at a blank page and write either too little or far too much, and the result reads like a list of dates or a sales pitch. To write an about page with AI writing tools is to shape a real story with a clear structure, honest words, and a reason for the reader to trust you. The tool does not invent your story, it helps you tell the one you already have.

1. Decide what the reader needs to know first

A visitor on your about page is asking one question: can I trust these people with my money and my time. Answer that question first, before the history and the mission statement. A visitor wants to know what you do, who you do it for, and why you are good at it, in that order. Everything else is background, and it can wait for the second half of the page.

Write down three facts about your business that a stranger would find reassuring: how long you have done this work, how many customers you have helped, and what makes your way of working different. If you have no big numbers yet, use honest ones, such as the year you started and the town you work in. These about page copy tips sound simple, but they decide whether a visitor keeps reading or clicks away.

2. Write an about page with AI writing tools, step by step

Start by telling the tool the plain facts, in your own words, without worrying about style. Write a few rough lines: when you started, why you started, what you do now, and who you serve. Feed those lines to the tool and ask for a short draft in a warm, plain tone, with short paragraphs and no hype. A draft built from your own facts will sound like you, while a draft built from a vague prompt will sound like everyone.

Read the draft aloud and mark every line that feels wrong, too grand, or simply untrue. Ask the tool to rewrite only those lines, keeping the rest. A good draft needs two or three rounds of small fixes, not one big rewrite. Our guide to rewriting and tightening a draft shows how to cut a loose draft down to the lines that matter.

3. Keep the story honest and specific

A story with real detail is easy to trust, and a story with vague claims is easy to doubt. Say the name of the town, the year you started, and the problem you first set out to solve, instead of writing about passion and excellence in general terms. A reader who sees a real place and a real date will believe the rest of the page, while a reader who sees only praise will wonder what is being hidden.

Avoid claims you cannot back up, such as best in the region or loved by thousands, unless you can show the proof on the same page. Honesty is not a weakness on an about page, it is the point. Business story writing works when the facts do the talking, and a modest claim with proof behind it will beat a grand claim with nothing behind it.

4. Shape the structure: problem, people, promise

A clear structure will carry a reader through the page without effort. Open with the problem your customers face, then introduce the people behind the business, then make a simple promise about how you work. This problem, people, promise shape fits almost any business, from a plumber to a bakery, and it keeps the page from wandering into a list of dates.

Keep each part short. The problem needs two or three lines, the people need a short paragraph each, and the promise needs one clear sentence. A reader should reach the end of the page in under a minute and know exactly what you do and why you care. About page examples structure follows this same shape again and again, because it matches the way a visitor actually reads.

5. Add proof without sounding like a sales pitch

Proof belongs on an about page, but it should read like evidence, not advertising. A short quote from a real customer, a photo of the team at work, or a number such as years in business or jobs finished will do more than a paragraph of praise. Place the proof near the claim it supports, so the reader sees the evidence at the moment they need it.

Use real names and real places where you can, with permission, because a quote from a named customer in a named town is far stronger than an anonymous line. Our guide to writing product descriptions that sell shows the same habit of pairing a claim with proof, and our landing page copy guide explains how proof turns a visitor into a customer.

6. Edit the draft until it sounds like you

Read the finished page aloud one more time, and listen for any line that you would never say to a customer face to face. If a line sounds like a brochure, rewrite it in the words you would use in a real conversation. A page that sounds like a person will be trusted, while a page that sounds like a template will be skimmed and forgotten.

Ask one honest friend to read the page and tell you what it says about you, not whether the grammar is correct. If their answer matches the business you run, the page is done. If it does not, the page needs one more round of edits, not a new tool. Our guide to writing business plan sections shows the same habit of testing a draft against the real business behind it.

Bottom line: To write an about page with AI writing tools is to start with the facts, shape them into a problem, people, promise structure, keep every claim honest and specific, add real proof, and edit until the page sounds like you. The tool does the shaping, the story stays yours, and the visitor who reads it will know exactly who you are and why you can be trusted.

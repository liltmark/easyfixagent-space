Using AI Chatbots Safely: What Never to Share and How to Protect Your Data

October 29, 2026 · AI Tools · 6 min read

A chatbot feels like a private conversation, but it is not quite the same as talking to a person in a closed room. What you paste may be stored, reviewed, or used to improve the service, depending on the tool and its settings. That does not mean you should avoid chatbots. It means you should know the line between safe material and sensitive material before you type, so you can get the help you want without handing over anything you would regret sharing.

1. The never-share list

Some things should never go into a chatbot, in any tool, on any plan. Passwords and login codes top the list, followed by bank details, card numbers, national ID numbers, and private keys or recovery phrases. Full home addresses, phone numbers, and dates of birth also belong here, as do medical records and anything that could identify a child. A useful test is simple: if you would not post it on a public noticeboard, do not paste it into a chat window. You can almost always get the same help without the sensitive detail, by describing the problem in general terms instead.

2. Work files need extra care

Personal caution is one thing; work material is another, because it may belong to your employer or your clients. Customer lists, unpublished prices, internal reports, and unreleased product details should not go into a consumer chatbot unless your employer has approved that tool for work use. Many workplaces now have a written AI policy, and it is worth reading before you upload a spreadsheet "just to summarise it".

When you do need help with a work document, strip it first. Replace real names with roles, round exact figures, and cut sections that are not relevant to your question. You will usually find the answer is just as good, because the chatbot is responding to the structure and wording of your question, not to the private facts inside it. Our guide to checking facts instead of trusting answers is also worth reading here, since work answers deserve verification before they go any further.

3. Check what the tool keeps

Not every chatbot treats your chats the same way. Some keep conversations for a set period, some use them to train future models, and some offer a setting to switch that off. The differences are usually spelled out in the privacy or data policy, and it is worth the five minutes to read the section on training and retention for the tool you use most. Paid and business plans often include stricter controls than free plans, which is one of the real differences between tiers, not just a marketing point.

Also check how long chats are stored and whether you can delete them. Most tools let you delete individual conversations or your whole history, and some offer a temporary chat mode that does not save anything. If you regularly discuss sensitive topics, make deleting old chats a habit, the same way you clear old downloads. Our guide to working around free-plan limits explains how free tiers handle history and storage, which affects privacy as well as convenience.

4. Use the settings that actually help

Once you know what a tool offers, turn on the protections that matter. If there is a switch to stop your chats being used for training, turn it on. If there is a temporary or private chat mode, use it for anything borderline. Review connected apps and permissions from time to time, and sign out on shared devices rather than leaving your account open. Two-factor authentication on your chatbot account matters too, because your chat history itself can reveal a lot about your work and life if someone else gets in.

5. Safer ways to get help anyway

Privacy does not have to mean giving up on useful answers. You can ask a chatbot to explain a topic, draft a template, or suggest an approach using invented examples instead of your real situation. "Write a polite reply to a customer asking for a refund" works just as well as pasting the actual complaint, and "explain this tax rule with a simple example" is safer than uploading your own figures. Learning to phrase requests this way is a skill in itself, and our basics of prompt engineering show how a well-written general prompt often beats a detailed private one.

6. If you already shared something

If you realise you pasted something sensitive, do not panic, but act quickly. Delete the conversation, and if the tool offers a way to request deletion of that data, use it. Then change any password or code that was exposed, even if you think nobody saw it, and keep an eye on the affected account for unusual activity. If the material belonged to your employer or a client, tell the right person promptly; covering it up usually makes the consequences worse, while an early report lets them take protective steps.

Finally, treat the incident as a prompt to set up better habits rather than a reason to stop using AI altogether. Decide your personal never-share list, write it down if it helps, and check your settings once a month. A short routine like this keeps the benefits of chatbots while keeping your private life and your work where they belong: with you. Bottom line: Never paste passwords, financial details, or other people's private information into a chatbot, check what your tool stores, and use general examples to get the same help safely.

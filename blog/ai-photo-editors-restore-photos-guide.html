Restore Old Family Photos with Free AI Tools: A Careful, Step-by-Step Guide

October 26, 2026 · Photos · 6 min read

Old family photos carry something no new photo can replace, which is exactly why restoring them feels risky. Done carelessly, AI tools can turn a grandparent's face into a stranger's. Done in stages, from a safe copy, they can bring back detail that time took away.

1. Start with a good scan and never touch the original

Restoration quality depends entirely on the scan. Scan at the highest resolution your scanner allows, save the file as a copy, and do all editing on that copy only. Keep the original scan untouched as your safety net. If a print is fragile or stuck in an album, photograph it in soft daylight rather than forcing it flat. A careful start prevents damage no AI tool can undo.

2. Fix damage before you enhance anything

AI enhancement applied to a scratched, stained photo just sharpens the damage. First remove the biggest problems: clean up dust and scratches, straighten the image, and crop away damaged borders. Only then move to colour and sharpness. Working in this order means each tool has a clean image to work from, and you will need far less aggressive enhancement later. Our free photo editor guide lists tools that handle cleanup and enhancement separately.

3. Enhance in small steps, checking faces constantly

Apply sharpening and upscaling gradually, and check faces at full size after every step. The moment skin starts looking plastic, eyes look too symmetrical, or a familiar face starts looking generic, you have gone too far. Step back one level. The goal is to recover what was there, not to invent a new person. This discipline is the whole difference between restoration and fabrication.

4. Treat colour with restraint

Colourising a black-and-white photo is tempting, but it is also guesswork: the tool cannot know what colour a dress really was. If you colourise, keep colours muted and natural, and always save a restored black-and-white version alongside it. For faded colour photos, restoring the original colours usually looks far more honest than inventing new ones. When in doubt, ask older family members what they remember before you decide.

5. Print and share with context

A restored photo gains meaning when it carries its story. When you share it, add what you know: who is in it, roughly when it was taken, and what was restored. Print restored photos at a size the resolution genuinely supports, rather than enlarging them until they blur. A smaller, sharp print honours the original far better than a large, soft one, and it will last longer in a frame.

6. Know what AI restoration cannot do

Some photos are beyond honest restoration: faces that are completely blurred, or large areas that are missing. In those cases, a partial restoration that keeps the damage visible is more truthful than a full invention. It is also worth remembering that restored images used in videos should be checked the same way: our image comparison explains why checking at full size matters before anything goes public.

7. Scan settings that actually matter

For prints, scan at 600 dots per inch if the scanner allows it; that resolution captures detail without creating enormous files. Save scans as TIFF or high-quality JPEG, and avoid scanning through glass that adds reflections. For negatives or slides, a dedicated film scanner or a scanning service is worth it, because phone photos of negatives rarely capture enough detail for a good restoration. The scan is the foundation, and no later step can add detail the scan never captured.

8. Organise restorations so family can find them

Name restored files clearly, with the person's name and approximate year, and keep them in a shared folder with the original scans alongside. Future family members will thank you for organisation more than for any single enhancement, because a restored photo nobody can find might as well not exist. Add a short text note with each folder explaining who is pictured and what is known about the occasion, while someone still remembers.

9. Print one, check it, then print the rest

Before printing multiple copies for family, print a single test copy at the intended size and check it in daylight. Colours on screen often differ from colours on paper, and a face that looked fine on a monitor can look too smooth or too sharp in print. Adjust once based on the test print, then print the batch. This saves paper, ink, and the disappointment of handing relatives a print that looked better on your screen.

Bottom line: Restore in stages, from a copy, and stop while the photo still looks like itself. A believable restoration beats a flawless-looking fake every time.

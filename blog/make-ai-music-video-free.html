How to Make an AI Music Video Free, Step by Step

November 1, 2026 · Guides · 6 min read
Written & tested by Muhammad Mudassir YaseenFounder, EasyFix Agent — every guide on this site is tested by hand before it is published.
🗓 Last updated: November 2026

A music video used to need a band, a camera crew and a budget. Now it needs three free tools and an afternoon: one to write the song, one to create the moving pictures, and one to stitch them together. This guide walks the whole chain in order — the exact workflow we use ourselves — with the honest limits of each free tier flagged as we go.

Quick answer

Write and generate your song on Suno's free plan. Take the song's cover art (or a still that fits the mood) into our free AI Video Studio and animate it, scene by scene. Then lay the clips over the track in a free editor, add captions, and export. Total cost: nothing. Total time for a first video: about an hour, most of it waiting on free generation queues.

Step 1: Make the song in Suno

Start where every music video starts — the track. On Suno's free plan you describe the song you want: a style ("slow acoustic love song, warm male voice"), a topic, and your own lyrics if you have them, or let it write lyrics from your idea. Generate two or three versions and keep the one whose chorus you catch yourself humming; a music video lives or dies on the hook. Download the audio file before you do anything else. Our Suno AI review covers the free plan's daily credits and quirks in full, but the one limit to hold in your head now is licensing: songs made on the free plan are for non-commercial use, which we come back to at the end.

Step 2: Plan the visuals against the lyrics

Listen to your song once with a notebook. Most tracks split naturally into four to six visual beats: an opening scene, a verse mood, the chorus moment, a bridge change, and a closing image. Write one line per beat describing a still picture — "a lighthouse at dawn, waves moving" beats "something ocean-y" every time. A short song needs roughly one animated clip every five to ten seconds, so a one-minute video means six to ten clips. That sounds like a lot, but each clip is one still image plus one sentence of motion, and you can generate them while the kettle boils.

Step 3: Create and animate the scenes in the studio

Open the EasyFix Agent Video Studio. You have two routes per scene. Route one: use the Image to Video tab, upload a still — your Suno cover art works beautifully for the opening shot — and describe the motion ("slow zoom in, light flickers gently, dreamlike"). Route two: use Text to Video and describe the whole scene in words for beats where you have no picture. Choose portrait (9:16) if the video is headed for Shorts, Reels or TikTok, landscape (16:9) for YouTube. Free engines cap anonymous use and queue at busy times; if a generation pauses, wait and retry, or paste a free Hugging Face token into the studio for your own private allowance. Our guide to animating a photo free online covers the motion-prompt technique in depth — one gentle movement per clip is the rule that saves your free generations.

Step 4: Stitch the video to the track

Open any free editor you already have — the editor on your phone, or a free browser editor — and lay the song down first. Then drop each clip onto the timeline where its lyric beat lands: chorus clip on the chorus, closing image on the final line. Trim clips to the beat rather than stretching them; a two-second clip placed exactly on a lyric change feels deliberate, while a long clip drifting across three lines feels like a screensaver. Add the lyrics as captions — most viewers watch on mute first, and captions are what turn a scroll-past into a full play. Our captions and subtitles workflow shows the fastest free way to do this without typing every line by hand.

Step 5: Export and post

Export at the shape you generated: 1080×1920 for vertical platforms, 1920×1080 for YouTube. Post the full song version to YouTube and a chorus-length cut to Shorts or Reels, pointing viewers to the full video. One production tip from experience: keep every still image, clip and the project file in one folder named after the song. Your second music video — and there usually is one, this process is addictive — will reuse half of it.

When you want a polish pass

If one scene deserves better than the free engines give — usually the chorus shot — that is the moment to spend a one-time free trial elsewhere rather than money. Our Runway AI review explains what its free credits buy and how its quality compares; a single cinematic chorus clip surrounded by studio clips lifts the whole video. And before you publish anything commercially, read the licensing position properly: our explainer on AI music licensing sets out what free-plan songs can and cannot be used for, in plain language.

FAQ

Is it really possible to make the whole video free? Yes — Suno's free plan for the song, our studio for the visuals, and a free editor for assembly. The real currency is time: free tiers queue and cap daily use, so a video may take an afternoon of short waits rather than one sitting.

Can I sell or monetise a video made this way? Be careful. Suno's free plan covers non-commercial use; monetised releases generally need a paid Suno plan for the song's commercial rights. The visuals you generate in our studio from your own prompts and images are yours to use, but the song is the part with strings attached.

My animated singer's face keeps warping. What fixes it? Ask for less: a still camera, gentle light movement, no head turns. Face-heavy clips are the hardest thing for free engines; cutting away to scenery on the difficult lines is what the pros quietly do too.

How long should my first AI music video be? Short. A 45–60 second song section — one verse and the chorus — is the perfect first project. You learn the whole workflow in an hour and actually finish, which matters more than length.

The verdict

Song in Suno, scenes in the free studio, assembly in any free editor: that is the whole recipe, and every step costs nothing but a little patience with free queues. Make the short version first, post it, and let the second video be the ambitious one.
